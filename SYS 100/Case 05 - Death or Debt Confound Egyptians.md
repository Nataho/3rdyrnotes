# Case 05 — Death or Debt Confound Egyptians

## Summary
Opens with the story of Heba Ibrahim, whose mother died of kidney failure the day Ibrahim's savings ran out — a picture of an Egyptian health system that "helps only those with means." The article uses her family's slide into debt and eviction to measure President Mohamed Mursi's first 100 days in office against his promises, finding an economy in its worst crisis in a decade, stalled politics, and rising disappointment with the post-revolution government.

## Storyline
1. The opening death: on a warm September morning — President Mursi's 74th day in office — Heba Ibrahim's mother breathes her last after five weeks of treatment for kidney failure, emaciated and comatose; the final breath comes the day the last of Ibrahim's savings runs out.
2. The accusation: a health-care system that "helps only those with means" — "Is this the new Egypt we were promised? A miserable country where the only thing left is to sell myself to raise money?"
3. The yardstick: Mursi, sworn in in June after squeezing past Ahmed Shafik, promised immediate fixes (streets, security, traffic) plus long-term goals (social justice, lower poverty) — and pledged to cure 64 of everyday life's ills within 100 days.
4. The scorecard: the "Mursi Meter" website, run by the unaffiliated nonprofit Zabatak, counts only four short-term pledges accomplished by the 100-day mark this week.
5. The street reacts: activist groups call a protest against overwhelming Islamist influence on the new constitution, parts of which were unveiled the day before.
6. The expert read: "Change hasn't brought any real benefits on the ground yet" — expectations run higher than the government's speed of delivery, "risking people losing hope that things will change" (Hirsh).
7. The economic bind: the worst crisis in a decade; a $4.8B IMF loan still unagreed; unemployment at 12.6% with 7.5% growth needed to cut it, versus a 4.5% target — after growth fell to 1.8% last year, a 19-year low.
8. The political vacuum: courts dissolved parliament; there's no constitution and no election date until one is drafted and ratified; blasphemy arrests fuel criticism that Mursi hasn't matched his freedom-and-dignity rhetoric.
9. The strategist's view: political goodwill is being banked "before the controversial economic decisions are taken" (Ziada).
10. The counter-frame: while analysts watch macro barometers, Egyptians — nearly half living on or below the poverty line — judge only tangible changes.
11. Mursi's Oct 6 speech (commemorating the 1973 war with Israel): a victory lap in an open-top vehicle around a Cairo stadium; he dismisses criticism of at least nine overseas trips as investment-hunting — "I still live in a rented apartment."
12. The claimed wins: forcibly retiring the two most powerful generals — "the second revolution" (Brotherhood spokesman Ghozlan) — plus unverifiable statistics claiming 70% of the 100-day security goals and majorities on bread, traffic, and fuel targets.
13. The street's rebuttal: bus driver Abdel-Halim Arafa, in hour two of a gas-station queue beside a mountain of uncollected garbage — "Really? … I hadn't noticed."
14. The medical bill of proof: striking doctors warn of a "health care collapse" and say 20 million Egyptians (about 1 in 4) have Hepatitis C; Ibrahim's ledger — more than 10,000 pounds for the five-week hospital stay, over 4,000 pounds owed to friends, nearly all her furniture sold, a 5,000-pound apartment deposit lost, eviction looming, no steady income.
15. The system's response: bureaucratic intransigence when she applied for state coverage of the costs — something she says was much easier under Mubarak.
16. The closing verdict: "Decisions are still made with the same authoritarian attitude" (activist Wael Khalil).

## Key Points
- **The 100-day scorecard:** Mursi pledged to fix 64 everyday ills; by his own milestone he had accomplished only four of the short-term pledges (tracked by the "Mursi Meter" run by nonprofit Zabatak). His claims of 70% success on security goals rang hollow to ordinary Egyptians.
- **Economy:** growth fell to 1.8% last year (a 19-year low), unemployment hit 12.6%, and the country needs 7.5% growth to bring joblessness down — versus a 4.5% target this year. A $4.8B IMF loan remained unagreed, starving social projects of funds.
- **Political drift:** parliament dissolved by courts, no constitution, no election date, blasphemy arrests, and protests planned against Islamist influence on the constitution draft.
- **Wins:** Mursi forcibly retired the two most powerful generals — described by the Brotherhood as "the second revolution" — ending the military council's grip.
- **Human cost:** Ibrahim paid over 10,000 Egyptian pounds for a five-week hospital stay, sold nearly all her furniture, lost a 5,000-pound deposit, owed friends 4,000+ pounds, and now faces eviction with no steady income. Striking doctors warned of a "health care collapse"; 20 million Egyptians (about 1 in 4) have Hepatitis C.
- **Sentiment:** nearly half of Egyptians live on or below the poverty line; an activist summed up — "Decisions are still made with the same authoritarian attitude."

## Analysis Questions (from the case)

2. Develop a reinforcing loop.
3. Create a balancing loop on top of the existing loop. Evaluate its significance.
