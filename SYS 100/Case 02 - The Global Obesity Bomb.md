# Case 02 — The Global Obesity Bomb

## Summary
A look at the global shift from the historic threat of *too little* food to the 21st-century threat of *too much*. Kicked off by NYC Mayor Bloomberg's proposed ban on soft drinks over 16 ounces, the article shows obesity spreading from the U.S. (the most obese industrialized nation) to developing countries, and argues the real problem is food distribution and rising incomes interacting with cheap, sugary food and sedentary work — a problem with no simple fix.

## Storyline
1. NYC Mayor Bloomberg's proposal to ban soft drinks over 16 ounces makes headlines — the latest front in his war on obesity, which kills 5,000 city residents a year.
2. The U.S. is the heavyweight champion of fat: the most obese industrialized nation, with two-thirds of adults overweight and one-third fully obese (WHO).
3. The twist: upstart challengers from the developing world — Panama, Saudi Arabia, and six Pacific Island nations — already have higher obesity rates than America. A historic tipping point: after millennia of fearing *too little* food, the 21st century fears *too much*.
4. The global data: worldwide obesity nearly doubled from 1980–2008; by 2008 roughly 502 million adults were obese, costing 36 million disability-adjusted life-years (Lancet).
5. The paradox: ~1 billion people may still be undernourished — but it's a distribution problem, not a supply problem. Evidence: 1 in 10 Russian households contains both underweight and overweight members; Sen's famine work shows the fix is simply money to buy food.
6. Poverty is falling ($1.25/day population halved since 1990), and the income–nutrition relationship breaks down around $5,000 average income (India $3,700 and Indonesia $4,700 just below; China $8,400 past it) — past that line, most people can eat enough and *choose* to eat too much.
7. Plenty has slack: a third of food is wasted; productivity gains (seed varieties, fertilizer micro-dosing, drip irrigation) make feeding 9 billion sustainable — the challenge is keeping fat from becoming the No. 1 killer.
8. Why obesity resists the old toolkit: measles had a vaccine, malaria bed nets, diarrhea sugar-water — obesity has a whole range of causes and no single solution.
9. The causal web: desk jobs cut the calories humans need; agricultural productivity slashed food costs; rising wealth buys sugary and fatty products; marketers follow the money; any country with a McDonald's is seeing obesity grow.
10. Two consoling notes: obesity is a disease of choice, and choices can be shaped — NYC's 2003 school sweet-drink ban coincided with a 5% drop in public-school childhood obesity over the last four years.
11. Close: the problem of global plenty is real, but it's still considerably better than the reverse.

## Key Points
- **U.S. scale:** two-thirds of adults overweight, one-third obese (WHO); obesity kills 5,000 NYC residents a year.
- **Global tipping point:** Panama, Saudi Arabia, and six Pacific Island nations already have higher obesity rates than the U.S. Worldwide obesity rates nearly doubled from 1980–2008; ~502 million adults obese, costing 36 million disability-adjusted life-years (Lancet).
- **Hunger persists by distribution, not supply:** ~1 billion undernourished while 2 billion+ eat too much; even 1 in 10 Russian households contains both underweight and overweight members. Amartya Sen: famine is usually solved simply by giving the poor money for food.
- **Income threshold:** the income–nutrition link breaks down around $5,000 average income (India $3,700, Indonesia $4,700, China $8,400) — past that, most people can eat enough, and choose to eat too much.
- **Plenty has slack:** one-third of food is wasted; productivity gains (fertilizer micro-dosing, drip irrigation) make feeding 9 billion people sustainable.
- **Why obesity is hard to fix:** falling calorie needs (desk jobs), falling food costs, growing wealth, aggressive junk-food marketing, and "eat less fat and sugar" messaging that doesn't work. It's a disease of choice — but NYC's 2003 school sweet-drink ban coincided with a 5% drop in public-school childhood obesity.
