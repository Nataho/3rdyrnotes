# Case 10 — Kodak Files for Bankruptcy as Digital Era Spells End to Film

## Summary
Eastman Kodak — the photography pioneer founded in 1880, inventor of the Brownie camera and Kodachrome film, and creator of the *first digital camera in 1975* (which it shelved to protect its lucrative film business) — filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. A classic case of disruptive technology and organizational inertia: Kodak invented the technology that destroyed its own business model and failed to commercialize it in time.

## Storyline
1. The filing: Eastman Kodak — the photography pioneer that introduced the Brownie Camera more than a century ago — enters Chapter 11 after consumers embraced digital cameras, a technology Kodak itself invented and failed to commercialize.
2. The balance sheet: assets of $5.1 billion against debt of $6.8 billion, filed in U.S. Bankruptcy Court in Manhattan; Moody's had cut ratings on ~$1B of Kodak debt on Jan 5 citing "a heightened probability of a bankruptcy over the near-term," and the NYSE says it will suspend trading as the company is "no longer suitable for listing."
3. The cultural autopsy: "They were a company stuck in time… their history was so important to them… Now their history has become a liability" (Robert Burley, Ryerson, who has photographed shuttered Kodak plants since 2005).
4. The deterioration: credit worsened as traditional film revenue tumbled, and the Instamatic maker was slow over the past decade to compete with Canon and Hewlett-Packard in digital cameras and printers.
5. The lifeline: Citigroup agrees to a $950M debtor-in-possession loan (pending a bankruptcy judge's approval); CEO Perez calls it "a significant step toward enabling our enterprise to complete its transformation," and significant asset sales are planned.
6. The shrinkage already endured: 47,000 employees cut since 2003; 13 film/paper/chemical factories and 130 photo laboratories closed; $3.4B spent on restructuring, done "in a socially responsible" way; NY Governor Cuomo pledges support for Rochester.
7. The decline in numbers: headed for a sixth annual loss in seven years; cash fell from $1.4B to $862M; revenue halved since 2005 to $7.2B with further declines forecast; film/photofinishing sales sank 14% in Q2; losses since 2008 exceed $1.76B.
8. The market's verdict: bonds fall 3.5 cents to ~29.5 cents on the dollar (from 100 cents in April, 48.5 in November); shares slump 35% to 36 cents and the ticker becomes EKDKQ.
9. The creditors: Bank of New York Mellon is both biggest unsecured creditor (trustee for ~$670M of notes) and biggest secured creditor ($776M claim backed by all U.S. assets except 1988 exemptions); Sony ($16.7M), Warner Brothers ($14.2M), and Alcoa ($2.8M) among the unsecured.
10. The turnaround that came late: Perez (a former HP executive, in charge since 2005) cut costs and won shelf space for inkjet printers at Wal-Mart and Staples while pushing commercial digital presses into publishing — but Kodak was "five years too late," and without enough installed printers and presses, ink, supplies, and service contracts can't end the losses (profit projected by end-2013).
11. The model problem: "they're moving away from a very profitable model that generated multiple sales — most everyone got double prints — to one that's awfully difficult to make a profit in" (Ward), and "there just weren't a lot of options to replace the film business."
12. The endgame: a much smaller company can emerge from bankruptcy, "but I really don't believe that there's going to be another Kodak moment" (former VP Don Strickland).
13. The patent war: selling 1,100+ digital-imaging patents while suing Samsung (Galaxy tablet infringement), HTC, Apple, RIM, and Fujifilm — Samsung had already paid $550M in 2010 to settle a dispute over an image-preview patent.
14. The legacy: Eastman's dry-plate method led to the 1888 Kodak camera, the invention of film (enabling Edison's motion picture camera), the $1 Brownie, and Kodachrome — immortalized in Paul Simon's 1973 song, production ended in 2009; Kodak products underpinned family photos, hospital diagnostics, book printing, police work, and Hollywood.
15. The fatal irony: Kodak invented the first digital camera in 1975 — and shelved it because it would threaten the lucrative film business; Perez's verdict: "phenomenal in research and patents and not so good commercializing things, actually terrible commercializing things."
16. The final reorganization: a chief operating office (Faraci on commercial, Quatela on consumer) replaces three divisions; digital operations already account for ~75% ($4.5B) of revenue; headcount is ~17,000 (9,100 in the U.S.) versus 63,900 in 2003.
17. The boardroom exodus: three directors resigned in December — KKR's Clammer and Chen (seated in 2009 after a refinancing deal of $300M in senior bonds plus warrants on 40M shares) and Laura Tyson, a director since 1997.

## Key Points
- **The numbers:** assets of $5.1B against debt of $6.8B; Citigroup provided a $950M debtor-in-possession loan. Revenue fell by half since 2005 to $7.2B; losses since 2008 exceeded $1.76B; cash fell from $1.4B to $862M in a year. Bonds fell to ~29.5 cents on the dollar; the stock dropped 35% to 36 cents and the NYSE moved to delist.
- **Shrinking:** Kodak shed 47,000 employees since 2003 (63,900 → ~17,000), closed 13 film/paper/chemical factories and 130 photo labs, at a restructuring cost of $3.4B done "in a socially responsible way."
- **The pivot that came late:** CEO Antonio Perez (ex-HP, joined 2005) pushed inkjet printers into Wal-Mart and Staples and commercial digital presses into publishing — but admitted Kodak was "five years too late." The company moved from a highly profitable consumables model (film, double prints) to a hard-to-profit digital one, without the installed base of printers to drive recurring ink and service revenue.
- **Monetizing patents:** selling 1,100+ digital-imaging patents; lawsuits against Samsung, HTC, Apple, RIM, and Fujifilm (Samsung had paid $550M to settle a 2010 dispute). Digital operations were already ~75% ($4.5B) of revenue.
- **Cultural diagnosis:** "They were a company stuck in time… their history has become a liability" (Robert Burley, Ryerson). Former VP Don Strickland: "I really don't believe that there's going to be another Kodak moment."
- **Legacy:** Kodak invented film that enabled Edison's motion picture camera, sold $1 Brownies, was immortalized in Paul Simon's "Kodachrome," and underpinned Hollywood, hospitals, and publishing — an example of the 20th-century brand collapsing in the digital era.
