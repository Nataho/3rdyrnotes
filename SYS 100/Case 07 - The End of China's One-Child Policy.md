# Case 07 — The End of China's One-Child Policy?

## Summary
A rising chorus of China's top demographers, sociologists, and economists is urging an end to the 1979 one-child policy. The 2010 census revealed a much steeper birth decline than expected, and the policy now threatens China's transition from "factory to the world" to an innovation economy: the workforce is aging fast, the demographic dividend is about to end, and the country is "getting old before it has gotten rich."

## Storyline
1. Molly Zhang's fine: a 31-year-old lighting-industry account manager has her second son and must pay ~30,000 yuan ($4,760) — roughly her annual salary — to get the household registration without which education and employment would be impossible.
2. The catalyst: publication of the 2010 census reveals a much more pronounced birth decline than estimated, galvanizing some 20 of China's top demographers, sociologists, and economists to petition top leaders twice to reform or end the policy (Gu Baochang: "China's situation has changed so much").
3. The latest salvo: April 16 publication of James Liang's book *Too Many People in China?* — three publishers refused it as too sensitive; its thesis: the aging workforce the policy created will stifle Beijing's goal of shifting from factory of the world to innovation economy (evidence from 60+ countries; Japan as the classic case of older workforce, lower entrepreneurship).
4. The establishment line: the policy, in place since 1979, has strong official backing — the ~500,000-employee National Population and Family Planning Commission claims it averted 400 million births, and on April 10 top leaders reiterate their intent to keep birthrates low.
5. The roots of the mindset: shortages and food rationing just after the Cultural Revolution ended in 1976; Wang Feng's diagnosis — policy makers' beliefs are "back like it was 30 to 40 years ago," imagining a Chinese people who naturally overbreed.
6. What the policy produced: average births per couple fell from about six (1966) to around 1.5 today; the population will likely peak at 1.4 billion before 2030 — versus roughly two children for U.S. couples.
7. The dividend expires: the growing labor force contributed ~0.9% to annual economic growth (World Bank); the working-age population peaks at 1 billion in 2013 and then shrinks — the 120 million-strong 20–24 cohort will drop more than 20% within a decade (UN).
8. The first casualties: labor shortages appear earliest in the Pearl River Delta, whose export factories relied on workers under 25; next comes the harder problem of employing mushrooming numbers of older workers with "much less human capital" (Banister).
9. The race lost: the U.S. and Europe took about 100 years to become aging societies; China did it in under 40 — "getting old before it has gotten rich" (O'Keefe, World Bank).
10. The loosening so far: ethnic minorities have long been exempt; farmers whose first child is a girl may have a second; most provinces now allow second children to couples who are themselves single children — yet about two-thirds of Chinese remain restricted, and enforcement still means fines or pressure toward abortion (Banister).
11. The tone shift: the People's Daily reports the retirement of the most threatening slogans — "We would rather scrape your womb than allow you to have a second child!" is out; elderly-allowance messaging is in.
12. The bottom line: demographers concede aging is irreversible (fertility now falls with wealth), but keeping the policy only "steps on the gas pedal" down a demographic downhill (Wang) — threatening the move from ordinary factory work to innovation.

## Key Points
- **The human face:** Molly Zhang, 31, must pay a ~30,000 yuan ($4,760) fine — about a year's salary — for her second son, to get the household registration her child needs for school and work.
- **The policy machine:** the National Population and Family Planning Commission employs about half a million people and claims the policy averted 400 million births on top of today's 1.34 billion population.
- **Dramatic demographic shift:** couples averaged about six children in 1966; the birthrate is now ~1.5 (U.S. ~2). China's population will likely peak at 1.4 billion before 2030.
- **The dividend runs out:** the growing labor force contributed ~0.9% to annual growth (World Bank). The working-age population peaks at 1 billion in 2013, then shrinks. The 20–24 age group (120 million) will fall more than 20% within a decade — labor shortages have already hit the Pearl River Delta export factories.
- **Aging before rich:** the U.S. and Europe took ~100 years to become aging societies; China took under 40 years. Older workers have less human capital to offer employers.
- **The innovation argument:** James Liang (Ctrip chairman, Stanford PhD) argues in *Too Many People in China?* that older workforces stifle entrepreneurship — citing Japan — and that the policy undermines Beijing's innovation goals. Three publishers refused the book for its sensitivity.
- **Half-steps and softening:** minorities, farmers whose first child is a girl, and couples who are both single children may have more; about two-thirds remain restricted. Slogans are being softened ("We would rather scrape your womb…" retired). Demographers concede aging is now irreversible — falling fertility follows wealth — but say keeping the policy only accelerates it.
