# Case 03 — The Financial Crisis Blame Game

## Summary
Written mid-2008 crisis, the article argues the collapse was a "disaster by committee" — not the work of a few villains, but the *whole system*: government, Wall Street, and ordinary borrowers all made mistakes. It walks through the leading scapegoats (regulators, Fannie Mae, Greenspan, borrowers themselves), then traces the structural mechanics: a housing bubble, securitization, derivatives, excessive leverage, and feckless regulators.

## Storyline
1. The blame game begins: CNN counts down the "10 Most Wanted Culprits of the Collapse," the NY Post prints "Fraud Street" over FBI investigations, Congress hauls executives into hearings, and candidates trade blame.
2. The suspect list assembles: Phil Gramm and Chris Cox (loosened regulation), Franklin Raines (Fannie Mae), Alan Greenspan (interest rates), or the borrowers themselves (no-doc, interest-only loans) — your villain depends on where you think the landslide began.
3. The verdict forming: "It really was the whole system" (Aggarwal); even Obama concedes "everyone was living beyond their means — from Wall Street to Washington to even some on Main Street."
4. Warning sign #1 — home prices: the median price-to-income ratio held at 2.9–3.1 for two decades, then hit 4.0 (2004) and 4.6 (2006); four in ten California homeowners spent 30%+ of income on housing; everyone shared a "false sense of security that housing prices would always go up."
5. Warning sign #2 — securitization: the Fed cut rates from 6.5% to below 2% by end-2001, driving investors into complex mortgage-backed securities; the "securitization pipeline" created pressure to originate loans, feeding subprime lending and an "upward spiral" of prices — brokers were "doing what the system was asking them to do."
6. Washington's co-sign: the Clinton and Bush administrations pushed homeownership for everyone, and Congress overlooked problems at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac until the government takeover in early September 2008.
7. The accountability gap: banks had no "skin in the game" — they sold the securities and suffered nothing if the loans exploded; ratings agencies blessed the packages, so no one vetted loan quality the way the old savings-and-loan officer did.
8. The casualties named: Lehman ($95.8B of MBS, top issuer of 2007) and Bear Stearns ($100B, top of 2006) — both failed in 2008.
9. The hidden amplifier: opaque credit default swaps and derivatives with no central marketplace turned one firm's losses into chain reactions — Buffett's 2003 "financial weapons of mass destruction."
10. The leverage bet: the SEC loosened rules in 2004 (from 10–15x to 30–40x leverage); bonuses ($23.9B industry-wide in 2006; Lehman's Fuld at $34.4M in 2007) rewarded short-term risk while risk managers were told not to "get too much in the way of generating revenue."
11. The regulatory void: no transparency into firms' exposures; warnings from lower-level officials ignored; Wall Street's $97.7M (2004) and $70.5M (2006) in political donations; the 1999 repeal of Glass-Steagall sent investment banks "operating more like hedge funds."
12. The stripped-down verdict: basic bad banking — "never borrow short to lend or invest long" — plus too much risk, and a system so incentive-aligned that "it was very difficult to stop."

## Key Points
- **Bubble warnings ignored:** the median home price-to-income ratio sat at 2.9–3.1 for two decades, hit 4.0 by 2004 and 4.6 by 2006; by 2006, 4 in 10 California homeowners spent 30%+ of income on housing. Everyone had a "false sense of security that housing prices would always go up."
- **Securitization monster:** the Fed cut rates from 6.5% to below 2% by end-2001, driving yield-hungry investors into complex, hard-to-price mortgage-backed securities. Subprime lending fed an "upward spiral" of home prices. Originators had no "skin in the game" — banks sold the risk onward.
- **Culprit firms:** Lehman Brothers issued $95.8B of MBS in 2007 (industry top) and Bear Stearns $100B in 2006; both collapsed in 2008.
- **Derivatives as amplifiers:** opaque credit default swaps with no central marketplace increased the chance of chain-reaction losses — Buffett's "financial weapons of mass destruction."
- **Leverage:** the SEC loosened rules in 2004, allowing investment banks to leverage 30–40x core holdings (previously 10–15x). Wall Street bonuses hit $23.9B in 2006; Lehman's CEO earned $34.4M in 2007.
- **Regulators didn't regulate:** a "free market philosophy" let things run; the securities industry gave $97.7M to federal candidates in 2004 and $70.5M in 2006; the 1999 repeal of Glass-Steagall blurred the line between commercial and investment banking.
- **Core lesson:** basic bad banking — "never borrow short to lend or invest long" — plus too much risk, and near-total silence from insiders who assumed someone smarter understood the instruments.
