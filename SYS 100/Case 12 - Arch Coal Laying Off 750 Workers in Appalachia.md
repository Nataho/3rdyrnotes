# Case 12 — Arch Coal Laying Off 750 Workers in Appalachia

## Summary
Arch Coal, one of the world's largest producers (16% of U.S. coal output in 2010), announced ~750 layoffs across Kentucky, Virginia, and West Virginia — the bulk (almost 600) in Kentucky — as utilities switch from coal to cheaper, cleaner natural gas. The article captures a system under pressure from both sides: market forces (cheap gas, low demand, depleted easy-to-mine seams) *and* regulation (EPA emission rules), with hard-hit Appalachian towns blaming policy while industry fundamentals point to a structural decline.

## Storyline
1. The announcement: on a Thursday, Arch Coal — one of the world's largest producers, with 16% of U.S. coal output in 2010 — says it will lay off about 750 workers in the Kentucky, Virginia, and West Virginia coalfields, the latest setback for an industry struggling to keep market share as utilities switch to cleaner, cheaper ways to generate electricity.
2. The geography of pain: almost 600 of the cuts are in Kentucky; the disappearance of high-paying mining work heightens anxiety in "hardscrabble" towns bracing for leaner budgets and longer unemployment rolls.
3. The local read: Perry County judge-executive Dennis Ray Noble calls it "just a start" — his county has lost about 30% of its mining jobs in the last year and joblessness stands at 12.4%.
4. The company's moves: subsidiaries will close three higher-cost mining complexes and their prep plants (two in Kentucky, one in West Virginia), temporarily idle another Kentucky complex, and curtail output at other facilities; CEO John Eaves cites "market pressures and a challenging regulatory environment" pushing U.S. coal consumption to a 20-year low, framing the cuts as necessary "to weather the current downturn and to position the company for long-term success."
5. The production math: the reductions trim thermal coal output by more than 3 million tons annually; 2012 thermal sales are guided at 128–134 million tons, with emphasis shifting to higher-margin metallurgical coal.
6. The market backdrop: coal's share of U.S. electricity is forecast to fall below 40% for the year — the lowest since data collection began in 1949, down from 50% four years ago, and likely near 30% by the end of the decade.
7. The substitute: utilities increasingly switch to natural gas, which has become cheaper as supplies grow and produces fewer emissions of toxic chemicals and climate-changing gases than coal.
8. The blame game begins: from Kentucky courthouses to Congress, officials blame tougher environmental rules; Knott County's judge-executive Thompson sees "the writing on the wall that it's getting too difficult to operate at a profit" (~250 jobs lost, 12.5% jobless), while coal severance tax revenues dwindle against rising demand for services.
9. The congressional version: Rep. Hal Rogers cites the mild winter, low thermal-coal demand, and cheap natural gas — but pins primary blame on the Obama administration's deadlocked mining permits, rule changes, and "crippling" power-plant regulations: it "has continually kicked the industry while it's down."
10. The scientific contrast: GAO figures — coal plants emit more than 90 times the sulfur dioxide (acid rain), five times the nitrogen oxide (smog), and twice the carbon dioxide (greenhouse gas) of gas plants.
11. The regulatory stack: two clean-air rules enacted over the past year tighten sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide limits and add mercury limits — an AP survey finds they will force 32–68 of the dirtiest and oldest plants to close within three years; and in March the EPA issued guidelines that could limit greenhouse-gas emissions from *new* plants.
12. The counter-argument: environmental lawyer Robert Ukeiley calls the rules "completely reasonable," decades in the making, a "moral no-brainer" — the real cause is that "cheap coal in central Appalachia has been mined out"; remaining seams cost more to extract and are less profitable, so the decline would happen "even if we had the most anti-environmental president of our history in place."
13. The EPA's position: coal is still expected to generate more U.S. electricity than any other fuel; the drivers are low natural-gas prices, low electricity demand, and rising coal prices — "market conditions in the power sector are driving business decisions that are completely independent from these long-overdue toxic pollution standards, which power plants do not need to meet until 2016."

## Key Points
- **The cuts:** closing three higher-cost mining complexes and prep plants (two in Kentucky, one in West Virginia), idling another Kentucky complex, trimming thermal coal production by 3M+ tons annually; 2012 sales target of 128–134M tons; shifting emphasis to higher-margin metallurgical coal.
- **Coal's fading share:** U.S. electricity from coal was set to fall below 40% for the year — the lowest since data collection began in 1949 (down from 50% four years earlier, and likely ~30% by 2020). CEO John Eaves cited "market pressures and a challenging regulatory environment" pushing consumption to a 20-year low.
- **Why gas wins:** GAO figures — coal plants emit 90x the sulfur dioxide, 5x the nitrogen oxide, and 2x the CO2 of gas plants. Growing gas supply made it cheaper and cleaner.
- **Local devastation:** Perry County lost ~30% of its mining jobs in a year (12.4% jobless); Knott County faced ~250 lost jobs (12.5% jobless); coal severance tax revenues dwindled just as demand for services rose. Officials called it "just a start."
- **The regulation fight:** Rep. Hal Rogers blamed the Obama administration's permit deadlocks and "crippling" rules; EPA clean-air standards are expected to close 32–68 of the dirtiest plants within three years, and new-plant greenhouse-gas guidelines loom. The EPA countered that market conditions (low gas prices, low demand, rising coal prices) — not standards plants needn't meet until 2016 — were driving decisions.
- **The structural view:** environmental lawyer Robert Ukeiley argues cheap central Appalachian coal is simply "mined out" — remaining seams are costlier to extract, and the decline would happen "even if we had the most anti-environmental president of our history in place."
