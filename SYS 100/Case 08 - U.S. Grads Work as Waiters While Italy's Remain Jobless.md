# Case 08 — U.S. Grads Work as Waiters While Italy's Remain Jobless

## Summary
Two portraits frame the global youth jobs crisis: Cinzia Oliva, a 35-year-old Italian with an art degree who hasn't held a year-round job in 12 years, and Devon Wright, a 22-year-old American history graduate who moved back in with his parents. The article contrasts the U.S. and European labor markets — American hire-and-fire flexibility preserves bounce-back prospects, while European job protections for older workers shut the young out — and warns of a "lost generation" facing permanently depressed earnings.

## Storyline
1. The twin portraits: Cinzia Oliva, 35 — an Italian art graduate who hasn't held a year-round job in the 12 years since leaving the University of Naples, now managing a bar near Rome; and Devon Wright, 22 — an American history graduate of Middlebury College, four months out, unemployed, moved back in with his parents in Queens. "Cinzia Oliva may be Devon Wright's worst nightmare."
2. Wright's experience paradox: every employer wants prior experience he can't get without a job — so he gives up the professional hunt and looks for short-order cook or bookseller work instead.
3. The macro read: Fed Chairman Bernanke calls persistently high unemployment a cause of "enormous suffering," while expressing confidence the jobless rate will eventually return to pre-recession levels as in past downturns.
4. The record: 16.8% of 16–24-year-old Americans were jobless in August — the 42nd straight month at or above 16%, the longest such stretch in postwar history (previous episodes: 8 months in 1975, 23 months in 1981–83).
5. The hidden statistic: 31.5% of college-educated 25–29-year-olds held jobs outside the college labor market in Jan–May 2012 — up from 26.1% in 2007 and 30.1% in 2010 (Fogg and Harrington, Drexel).
6. Wright joins that group: taking interim customer-service or diner work "to get by" — "I need the money."
7. The policy response: persistently high joblessness pushes the Fed into a third round of quantitative easing this month, with a pledge to expand record stimulus if conditions don't improve.
8. Bernanke's Jackson Hole warning (Aug 31): stagnation "wreak[s] structural damage on our economy that could last for many years."
9. Europe's deeper hole: young Europeans battle the same troubles plus labor rules that favor older workers; Blanchflower's work suggests the young jobless may never catch up on wages.
10. The lost-generation warning: ManpowerGroup CEO Joerres predicts the same result as Europe — "a lost generation and an inability to get back those earnings," with productivity loss and social costs to worry about.
11. The scarring mechanism: Clemson's Mroz — graduates locked out "miss out on all that time to learn how to be a good worker"; office norms and customer skills are learned by practice, not school.
12. The employer's stake: a future shortage of proficient, motivated workers and first- and second-line managers — "You need to act quickly to get these young people some job experience."
13. Europe's numbers: Italy's 15–24 jobless rate hits 35.3% in July, nearly doubled in five years and past the 1990s highs; EU-wide youth unemployment stands at 22.5%, the highest since the euro's creation in 1999.
14. Oliva's story concludes: her six-month campground bar contract ends in October; 12 years of rejections — "a handful of available positions and thousands of people like me" — have killed the art/tourism dream: "I probably wouldn't have attended university in the first place."
15. The transatlantic difference: U.S. hire-and-fire flexibility versus European job protections — Italy's reformed 1970 labor code still lets fired workers win their jobs back in court if dismissal was "patently unfounded" (restored after a general strike); Fiat CEO Marchionne faces 70+ union legal actions and scales back Italian investment; France's 3,200-page Code du Travail is the biggest hiring obstacle; Hollande asks unions for flexibility while strengthening firing protections.
16. The counterweight: Johnson (MIT) — American fluidity preserves room to bounce back; "It's still too early to call this a lost generation in the U.S."
17. The cautionary data: 1980s-recession Britons unemployed by age 23 still reported lower wages and happiness at 50 (Blanchflower) — "It's really important for a young person to make the transition from school to work."
18. The endings: Oliva settles into bartending summers and waiting for the next; Wright and his friends resign themselves — "I don't want to say give up your hopes, but you have to resign yourself to the fact that this is how it is."

## Key Points
- **U.S. youth unemployment:** 16.8% of 16–24-year-olds were jobless in August 2012 — the 42nd straight month at or above 16%, the longest such stretch in postwar history (previous episodes: 8 months in 1975, 23 months in 1981–83).
- **Underemployment of grads:** 31.5% of college-educated 25–29-year-olds held jobs outside the college labor market in Jan–May 2012, up from 26.1% in 2007 (Drexel Center for Labor Markets and Policy).
- **Fed response:** Bernanke called joblessness a source of "enormous suffering" and warned of lasting structural damage, rolling out a third round of quantitative easing.
- **Europe's worse crisis:** Italy's 15–24 jobless rate hit 35.3% in July (nearly doubled in five years); EU youth unemployment was 22.5%, the highest since the euro's creation in 1999.
- **The rigidity mechanism:** legal protections make older workers nearly unfireable (Italy's reformed labor code still lets fired workers sue their way back; Fiat faces 70+ union legal actions; France's 3,200-page Code du Travail), so firms avoid hiring the young.
- **Scarring effects:** a Blanchflower study of 1980s-recession Britons found those unemployed by 23 still reported lower wages and happiness at age 50. Employers worry about a future shortage of experienced first- and second-line managers.
- **Why the U.S. differs:** fluid hiring/firing lets young workers re-enter and bounce back — "still too early to call this a lost generation in the U.S." (Simon Johnson).
